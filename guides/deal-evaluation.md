# Deal Evaluation Framework

## Compare landed cost, not sticker price

Use:

`landed cost = bottle price + shipping + tax + temperature-control / packaging fees + unavoidable handling`

This prevents a common comparison error: treating a low advertised bottle price as a bargain while ignoring expensive shipping or protection charges.

## Verify destination eligibility before counting shipping

A merchant banner such as “free shipping” does not prove that a specific wine can legally or operationally be delivered to a specific destination.

Before treating shipping as free or even available:

1. add the exact bottle to cart;
2. enter the actual destination;
3. verify that checkout offers a valid delivery method;
4. only then include the quoted shipping cost in the comparison.

If checkout is pickup-only, evaluate the bottle as a **pickup purchase**, not as a free-shipping purchase.

## Pickup has a practical cost

For pickup-only wine, a more realistic comparison is:

`practical cost = bottle price + tax + travel / tolls / parking + time / inconvenience`

The exact value of time is subjective, but the framework prevents a small sticker-price advantage from being mistaken for a real bargain.

A useful distinction:

- **dedicated pickup:** the bottle must justify the trip on its own;
- **incremental pickup:** once a worthwhile pickup trip is already planned, adding another genuinely attractive bottle has much lower marginal logistics cost.

Low marginal pickup cost should not be used to rationalize mediocre bottles.

## Consolidation

When merchant policy and weather allow, consolidating several bottles can materially reduce freight cost per bottle. The trade-off is that longer holding periods can introduce inventory, weather, or cash-flow considerations.

The same logic applies to pickup: consolidating several genuinely worthwhile bottles can reduce practical cost per bottle.

## Buy by role, not only by nominal quality

Not every bottle serves the same purpose. Separate at least:

- daily / casual drinking;
- mature drinking;
- benchmark or exploratory tasting;
- long-term cellaring;
- presentation / gifting.

A young, easy-drinking prestige Champagne may be excellent value as a daily bottle even if it is not especially memorable. A mature or historically important bottle should instead justify itself through complexity, condition, provenance, scarcity, or distinctive drinking experience.

Comparing these roles directly can create false “better/worse” conclusions.

## Merchant qualities that matter

Useful evaluation criteria include exact vintage/edition identification, credible provenance and storage history, bottle-condition disclosure for mature wine, temperature-aware shipping, hold-for-weather capability, consolidation flexibility, transparent pre-arrival terms, cancellation/substitution policy, pickup practicality, and total landed or practical price relative to reputable alternatives.

## Older bottles

For mature Champagne, Bordeaux, Burgundy, or sweet wine, provenance can dominate a small headline-price difference. Condition, storage history, fill level, label/capsule condition, and seller reputation may be worth paying for.
