# Mature Bottle Provenance

This page distills issues that came up in prior discussions of older Château d'Yquem and mature Champagne.

## Vintage reputation is not enough

For an older bottle, the specific bottle matters as much as the headline vintage.

The recurring checks were:

- provenance and storage history;
- fill level where relevant;
- capsule/cork condition;
- signs of seepage or heat exposure;
- seller reputation;
- whether the price still makes sense after shipping and tax.

## Ask about the history before the merchant acquired it

A statement such as “stored in our temperature-controlled facility” only describes the bottle **after it reached the current seller**.

For mature bottles, ask separately:

- where the bottle was sourced: private cellar, distributor, auction, another merchant, estate release, etc.;
- whether any earlier storage history is known;
- whether it has changed hands multiple times;
- whether the seller has inspected it for seepage, pushed cork, capsule damage, or other concerns.

Unknown pre-acquisition history is not automatically disqualifying, but it should be treated as uncertainty rather than silently converted into “good provenance.”

## Ask for actual-bottle evidence

For a specific mature bottle, stock photography is not enough.

Useful actual-bottle evidence includes:

- front and back label;
- capsule and cork area;
- fill level where visible and relevant;
- signs of seepage or staining;
- label damage;
- bottle color / glass and any visible sediment where informative.

For a one-off or very old bottle, actual-bottle photos are often more useful than generic tasting notes.

## Pickup can improve inspection, not provenance

Pickup can be attractive for very old or fragile bottles because it avoids parcel handling and allows condition to be checked before transport.

However:

- seeing the bottle in person does not establish its historical storage;
- a clean label does not prove sound provenance;
- an unattractive label does not necessarily imply damaged wine.

Keep **cosmetic condition, physical bottle condition, and provenance** as separate judgments.

## Shipping is part of provenance

Weather-sensitive shipping was repeatedly discussed.

Useful conclusions:

- avoid exposing valuable bottles to hot-weather ground shipping when a hold or temperature-controlled option is available;
- compare the landed cost of faster/cold-pack shipping rather than treating shipping as an afterthought;
- a slightly cheaper bottle can become the worse purchase if safe shipping is much more expensive;
- when delivery is unavailable, evaluate pickup logistics explicitly rather than assuming advertised shipping terms apply.

## Half bottles of d'Yquem

375 ml d'Yquem was specifically attractive because the wine is rich and normally poured in smaller quantities.

The format can make an older or expensive bottle easier to open without needing a large group.

## Bottle condition vs. packaging

For drinking, bottle condition and provenance matter more than cosmetic packaging.

Gift boxes can still matter for presentation or collecting, but they should not be confused with wine condition.

## Conclusion

For a mature bottle intended to be drunk, prioritize:

1. trustworthy provenance and condition;
2. the vintage and maturity profile;
3. total landed or practical cost;
4. packaging only if it matters for the intended use.

For expensive mature bottles, the best pre-purchase question is not merely “was it stored properly?” but **“what is known about the bottle before you acquired it, and can I see the exact bottle I would receive?”**

## Scope

Do not broaden this page into a generic auction or cellar-inspection manual unless those topics are separately researched in conversation.
